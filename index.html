Overview

Human Detection of Deepfake Face Images

Generative models have evolved from GAN-based architectures to diffusion models and multimodal large language models. We investigate how human detection performance differs across these generators, how training can help, and whether the effects persist over time.

We introduce the Deepfake Face Artifact Taxonomy (DFAT), a structured vocabulary of human-recognizable artifacts in generated faces. DFAT covers four domains: face, style, background, and accessories.

Our online experiment involved 524 participants and five generative models: StyleGAN2, StyleGAN3, GRAM-HD, Stable Diffusion 3.5, and image generation through the GPT-5 interface. We compared general-purpose and model-specific training, followed by a five-month follow-up with 342 participants.

Key Findings

  • Detection performance is model-dependent. In the sampled images, SD3.5 and GPT-5 faces were easier to detect than StyleGAN2 and StyleGAN3 faces.
  • Taxonomy-guided training improves detection. Accuracy increased significantly in 13 of 20 experimental conditions, with model-specific guidance especially useful for single-image tasks.
  • Training effects persist over time. No significant accuracy decline was found in the 20 paired comparisons at five months after Holm correction.

Study Design and Findings

Training Effectiveness

We tested detection before and after training using two presentation formats: a single image, and a side-by-side pair containing one real and one generated face.

Model-specific training generally produced greater numerical gains. Its clearest advantages over general-purpose training appeared for SD3.5 and GPT-5 in the single-image task.

For GAN-based models, direct differences in gains between training types did not remain significant after Holm correction.

Overall, 13 of 20 conditions showed a significant pre–post accuracy gain.

Abstract

Generative models for deepfakes have rapidly evolved from GAN-based architectures to diffusion models and multimodal large language models (MLLMs). However, empirical research on human performance in detecting deepfake face images has not kept pace. It remains unclear if (and how) detection performance varies across generative models and whether model-specific training improves detection more effectively than general-purpose training.

In this paper, we propose the Deepfake Face Artifact Taxonomy (DFAT), a cross-model taxonomy of human-recognizable visual artifacts in deepfake face images, based on which we build both general-purpose and model-specific training materials. Through an online experiment (n = 524), we examined human performance in detecting deepfake face images and evaluated training efficacy across five representative generative models: three GAN-based, one diffusion-based, and one MLLM (GPT-5). Our results show that participants’ detection accuracy was strongly model-dependent and that images generated by more recent and advanced models were not necessarily harder to detect. After training, participants achieved significant performance gains across most conditions, and their improvement varied by model. Notably, model-specific training generally yielded greater numerical gains than general-purpose training.

In a five-month follow-up test (n = 342), we observed no significant decline in detection accuracy and no significant increase in perceived stress. Overall, our findings provide actionable insights for designing effective, sustainable, and targeted training programs to counter the growing threat posed by deepfakes.
